## The relationship

AI is a tool: like any tool, it can be used well or poorly.
For a tool to be useful, it must first be understood. Different harnesses, models and prompts have different
strengths and weaknesses.

LLMs are nothing more than _copilots_ _(pun intended)_.

- They can generate ideas, but cannot _choose_ between them.
- They can write code, but cannot _decide_ what code should do.
- They can suggest improvements, but cannot _judge_ whether they are improvements.
- They can surface patterns, but cannot _know_ which patterns are relevant.

This is a deliberate division of labor. It might often be inconvenient, but it is also the only way to use these tools responsibly.

## The job

So, we've established that AI is a user-driven tool, and that it is not a replacement for human judgment. What does that mean in practice? How do I use AI in my work?

- **The boring parts.** LLMs shine at repetitive, mechanical work that doesn't require judgment: route shells, CSS module skeletons, bulk mechanical changes,
  renames, refactors, PR descriptions, commit messages. Necessary work that teaches nobody anything.
  Delegating this work to a model frees me to focus on the parts of the job that require ==judgment, taste, and experience==.

- **Prototyping.** Sometimes an idea is better in your mind rather than in reality.
  To know if it is worth building, the fastest path is a throwaway version: the model scaffolds the experiment, I poke at it in the browser,
  and decide if it's worth developing further.
  Most prototypes die there, but an LLM lets you explore more ideas in the same amount of time, and the ones that survive are usually of higher quality.

- **Performance investigations.** Sometimes you run into a weird performance problem that you can't explain.
  The work around it is simple, but sometimes tedious: measure, form a theory, test it, repeat.
  AI writes the measurement harnesses and performs sanity checks over re-renders, bundle sizes, and layout thrashing.
  A model _can_ lie, but cold, hard data and a profiler don't.

- **Analysis.** AI is incredibly good at holding large amounts of
  code in view and surfacing the structure in it. Reading unfamiliar code from third-party libraries, tracing where a bug stems from and mapping
  the flow of data through a system are all tasks an LLM can accelerate.

  When I hit a bug that I can't immediately understand, I usually ask the model to explain the logic involved.
  Once I have a grasp of the problem, I can pick one of two paths:
  - Investigate the bug myself, using the model as a sounding board for my reasoning.
  - Let the model do that, and then verify its reasoning and conclusions.

  Models can still end up with a plausible-sounding answer that is wrong, so nothing acts on my behalf before I've read it myself.
  The model is a tool for _understanding_, not a replacement for it.

- **Challenging the agent.** There are two things that I hate to hear from a model:
  - "You're absolutely right!" even when I'm not
  - "No, you're wrong!" even when I am right

  Not all models are trained to be agreeable, ==but that doesn't mean that they're right when they disagree==.
  The most useful sentence in my vocabulary when working with
  AI is some version of **"are you sure?"**

  Most of the time they'll be correct (even if only _partially_) and "not always right" is reason enough to keep my guard up.
  I make a habit of arguing both sides, asking for the strongest case against whatever was
  just proposed (including against my own idea), and demanding alternatives before
  accepting one.

  Sometimes the model defends its answer well and teaches me something.
  Sometimes it caves and reveals the first answer was luck or a wrong assumption.

  You're always right to push back, _ever so slightly_.

- **Offering insights and second opinions.** Explaining a design decision out loud to
  something that asks annoying questions catches half the bad ones before they land in
  git. "Why not just...?" from an LLM is worth as much as "why not just...?" from a
  colleague, and it's available at 2am.

- **Proofreading.** English is not my first language. While my understanding of the language is good enough, I may still
  fail to convey my ideas clearly, especially when the argument is technically complex, and not easily expressed in a single sentence.
  LLMs are good at proofreading and can help by drawing parallels, finding better ways to express ideas, and spotting inconsistencies in the text.

## The line drawn

- **Vibecoding.** Models can be prompted in a way that makes them self-sufficient on tasks, from start to finish.  
  Vibecoding is a seductive trap that forces you to trust the model's judgement even for calls that haven't been made yet:
  ==a codebase you haven't written is a codebase you can't maintain, debug, or improve: you simply don't own it anymore, the model does==.

- **Decisions.** What to build, what to cut, what "good" looks like. I make those calls, and I own the consequences when
  those decisions are wrong. A model can help me explore the options, but it should not make the call.

- **Unreviewed output.** Nothing merges because a model wrote it confidently.
  _Confidence_ is not _correctness_, and models have plenty of the former to spare.

## The deal

If you find something clever here, _I_ chose it. If you find a bug, it probably had
help being brought to life.

I use these tools aggressively for _leverage_ and conservatively for _authorship_, and I feel
like this is the right balance to use them responsibly. But still, YMMV, and so might mine one day.
